Fitness for Service versus Code Compliance: Understanding the Difference

Code compliance and Fitness-for-Service answer related but different questions. Compliance checks whether defined requirements are met; FFS evaluates whether equipment with a known condition can safely continue operating under stated loads and safeguards.

Construction and in-service context

Construction codes establish design, fabrication, examination and testing requirements for new equipment. In-service codes govern inspection and repairs in applicable services. Equipment can remain compliant with an inspection programme while still requiring engineering evaluation of a specific flaw. Where information is incomplete, the uncertainty should be recorded and addressed through verification, conservative assumptions, additional monitoring or appropriately timed examination.

In practical application, construction and in-service context should be translated into documented requirements for the relevant assets and responsible teams. For this subject, the priority is to ensure that fitness for service vs code compliance leads to an auditable decision, appropriate field or engineering activity, and a defined response when results fall outside the approved basis. Records should remain linked to the asset so future reviews can compare assumptions with actual condition and operating experience.

Acceptance criteria have boundaries

A fabrication acceptance criterion may not represent the remaining structural capacity of equipment after years of operation. Conversely, passing a local FFS calculation does not demonstrate compliance with every statutory, inspection or repair obligation. Applicable codes and standards provide important structure, but their relevance, edition and acceptance requirements must be confirmed for the asset, jurisdiction and client-approved scope.

What an FFS assessment adds

FFS uses actual flaw geometry, material properties, loads and damage mechanism to evaluate remaining margins. It can consider local metal loss, cracks, distortion and other conditions through defined procedures. Assumptions and limitations are integral to the conclusion. The strongest result is a traceable connection between the observed condition, its engineering significance and the action that protects continued operation.

Repair decisions

A condition may be fit temporarily but still require permanent repair for lifecycle, regulatory or operational reasons. Repairs and alterations should follow the applicable in-service code, design requirements, welding controls, examination and documentation. For UAE and GCC facilities, the final approach should also reflect local regulatory obligations, the owner’s engineering practices, environmental exposure and the practical access available during operation or shutdown.

Avoiding common misuse

Do not treat FFS as a waiver, or code minimum thickness as an automatic shutdown point without confirming the governing calculation. Keep inspection findings, code evaluation, engineering assessment and owner acceptance clearly separated in the record. The technical basis should identify the affected equipment, assumptions, uncertainties, responsible authority and evidence required to close the resulting action.

A defensible decision path

Identify the governing requirements, characterise the condition, determine whether direct acceptance applies, and escalate to FFS when necessary. Record operating conditions, safeguards, reassessment date and the authority approving continued service. This keeps the work focused on a decision and prevents a completed inspection or calculation from being mistaken for a fully controlled integrity outcome.
